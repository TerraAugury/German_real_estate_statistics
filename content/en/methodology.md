## Links

A Link relates one real estate KPI (key figure) to one economic indicator (a figure from the wider economy) over a stated period. It shows how the two series moved together. It does not show that one causes the other, and it is not a forecast.

Each Link has three parts:

- **The two series on one chart.** The KPI and the indicator are drawn together over the same time span. The KPI uses the left axis and the indicator uses the right axis. Some indicators come out more often than the KPI, for example monthly against quarterly. We average them to match the KPI, as described under Frequency. So the indicator line is our own calculation, not the figure the source published.
- **How closely they move together.** We compare *changes*, not the values themselves. For each series, we look at how much it rose or fell compared with a year earlier. For indices (series scaled to a base year) and money amounts, that is a percent change. For interest rates and confidence balances, it is a difference in percentage points. (A percentage point is the plain difference between two percentages.) Then we ask whether the two tend to rise and fall together.

  The answer is one number between −1 and +1, called Spearman's rank correlation. Near +1, the two tend to move the same way. Near −1, they tend to move opposite ways. Near 0, there is no clear pattern.

  We always show *n*, the number of periods compared, and the time span.

  We do not show significance tests. A significance test is often shown as a *p-value* or as stars (\*). It tries to say how likely it is that a pattern this strong appeared by pure luck. We leave it out on purpose. A star can look like proof. With only a handful of years or quarters, and several shifts tried, a good-looking result is easy to get by chance. So we show the number itself, how many periods it rests on (*n*) and the time span. You can then judge how much weight it deserves.
- **Did one move before the other?** Sometimes one series turns first and the other follows. To test this, we shift the indicator back or forward in time and repeat the comparison each time. For quarterly KPIs, we shift by whole quarters (up to 8 either way). For annual KPIs, we shift by whole years (up to 3 either way).
  - A **positive shift** means we compared the indicator with the KPI *later* in time, so the indicator moved earlier. Example: at +2 quarters, we set the indicator's change against the KPI's change two quarters afterwards.
  - A **negative shift** is the reverse: the indicator moved after the KPI.
  - The main figure is the one with **no shift**. It is written above the chart, and its bar is the one in the middle, under the 0 mark. The other bars show how the match changes as the shift changes.
  - We mark the shift whose number is **furthest from zero**: the strongest match in either direction. Be careful with this mark. We picked it from several shifts after seeing the results. So it is the best-looking of several tries, and it probably looks stronger than it really is. A line under the chart says so. It also gives the number of shifts that had enough data to be counted.
  - We never present a shift as a lead time or an advance warning. It describes the past only.

**Frequency.** A Link follows the KPI's own rhythm, quarterly or yearly. We never invent in-between values. We average monthly and quarterly indicators over each quarter or calendar year. We leave out a quarter or year with missing data.

**Minimum overlap.** A comparison on too few periods can mislead. Each shift needs at least 40 periods for quarterly KPIs or 10 for annual ones. Below that, we leave the shift blank. We show a Link only if the comparison with no shift reaches that minimum, and at least three shifts in all do. Otherwise the panel says "Link not shown" and gives the number of pairs it had with no shift. For annual Links with fewer than 20 pairs, we add a note that the pattern may be coincidental.

**The shift selector on the Links page.** Above the table of all Links, one selector sets the shift for the whole table at once. Its choices are "Same year", "Indicator 1 year earlier (N-1)" and "Indicator 2 years earlier (N-2)". These are the +1 and +2 shifts in the bar chart of shifts on each KPI page. So every box shows the same number as that bar, with the same count of pairs. (For a quarterly KPI they would be +4 and +8 quarters.) A box under the minimum overlap at the chosen shift shows "Not shown". The page opens on N-1, the pre-set shift described next. The choice is kept in the page address (#lag-0 for the same year, #lag-2 for N-2), so you can share a view. With no address part, the table shows N-1.

**A pre-set shift for appraisal-based KPIs.** Initial yield and the commercial property price index rest largely on appraisals (expert valuations). Appraisals follow interest rates with a delay. We compare these two KPIs with the 10-year Bund yield (the interest rate on 10-year German government bonds), Euribor 3 months and the loan rate. For these pairs, we set one year earlier (N-1) in advance. We did this on economic grounds, not from the results. These boxes are marked "pre-set shift" whichever shift is selected. On the Link panels, the +1 bar carries the same label, apart from the "strongest" mark. Switching between shifts and keeping the one that looks strongest overstates the link, for the same reason as the "strongest" mark.

**Grey boxes and the band on the bars.** A correlation based on few years can look strong by chance. So for every box we work out a band of ±2/√(n−1), using that box's own n at the chosen shift. A box whose number lies inside the band is shown in grey, and the number stays visible. The same band is drawn as a light strip on the bars of each Link panel. This is a rough guide, not a significance test. We show no stars and no p-values, for the reasons given above. For quarterly KPIs, each change is compared with a year earlier. So neighbouring quarters overlap and are not independent. The band then uses an adjusted n of n ÷ 4, rounded down, and the panel shows both numbers.

**Many boxes at once.** The Links page shows many comparisons together. So a few boxes will look strong by chance alone, just as several shifts tried on one Link produce a good-looking one. Treat a single strong box with caution, especially when n is small.

**Nominal and real.** Nominal means not adjusted for inflation. Real means adjusted for inflation. Rents and transaction volume are in nominal euros. So are the consumer price index and construction prices. Their Links to those two indicators therefore partly reflect shared inflation. Real GDP (gross domestic product, the value of all goods and services produced) is adjusted for inflation. So a link between a nominal KPI and real GDP compares nominal with real. The rent and transaction volume rows on the Links page carry a note saying so, and so do their Link panels against these three indicators. We do not adjust the figures for inflation in a Link.

**Rows and areas.** The rows of the Links page cover different Segments (property types) and areas. Each row starts with a tag naming its own: commercial overall or office, Germany or the 7 major cities, and for initial yield office and retail combined. Every indicator is national. So reading down a column compares unlike things.

**Geography.** Every indicator is national. On a City class or Land view, the Link compares the national indicator with that geography's KPI.

## The eight indicators

1. 10-year Bund yield (Deutsche Bundesbank), monthly.
2. Euribor 3 months, monthly average (European Central Bank). It is the rate at which banks lend to each other for 3 months. We use it as a stand-in for the ECB policy rate (the ECB's main interest rate).
3. Interest rate on new loans to non-financial corporations (companies outside the financial sector) over EUR 1 million, with the rate fixed for over 5 years (Deutsche Bundesbank), monthly. Commercial property is mostly financed at long fixed rates. So we use loans with a long fixed rate, not the floating rate.
4. Consumer price index (Destatis), monthly. It follows the prices of a basket of goods that households buy.
5. Real GDP (Deutsche Bundesbank), quarterly.
6. Registered unemployment rate (Deutsche Bundesbank), monthly.
7. Construction price index (Destatis), quarterly: office buildings for office views and for construction of office buildings, industrial and commercial buildings for all others.
8. OECD business confidence (OECD), monthly: retail for retail views, construction for all others. These series are not seasonally adjusted, so regular yearly swings stay in. They are percentage balances, so their changes are differences in percentage points.

## Insights

An Insight puts several series on one chart over a stated period. It adds a fixed one-line reading. Like a Link, it describes what the lines show. It does not show a cause, and it is not a forecast. Unlike a Link, it has no correlation and no shift.

- **One value per year.** An Insight uses yearly values. We average monthly and quarterly series over each calendar year. If a year is missing any months or quarters, we leave that year out instead of averaging part of it.
- **Period.** The period is the set of years that all the series cover. The average and the usual spread (the standard deviation, which shows how much values usually vary) use all of these years. An Insight needs at least 10 years. With fewer, the site says "Insight not shown" and tells you how many years it had. With fewer than 20 years, a note warns that the average rests on little data.
- **Big number.** The large number is one of these:
  - the gap between the main line (the KPI) and the comparison line (the reference series), in percentage points;
  - the KPI divided by the reference series (two indices with different base years are first set to 100 in the same year);
  - the gap between the two series' percent changes from the year before;
  - the percent change from the year before of one series;
  - for two Insights, the latest value of one series against its own average over the period (completions in percent, vacancy in percentage points).

  Measures based on change lose the first year. Gaps and growth rates show one decimal. Ratios show two.
- **Real figures.** A real rent is the rent index divided by the consumer price index. We then compare it with the year before. Rent is paid over the whole year, so we use the yearly average of the consumer price index. "Real office rents vs real GDP" compares this real rent growth with real GDP growth, so both sides are free of inflation. A price at one moment in time, such as a property price, would use the December index.
- **Real construction costs.** The construction price index (office buildings) is quarterly. The consumer price index is monthly. We average each over the calendar year, and we drop a year with a missing quarter or month. The big number is the real growth of construction prices, in percent: (1 + construction price growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1. The average, the verdict and the background colour follow it, and lower is better. The chart above it is the real construction cost index. It sets both indices to 1992 = 100. It compounds each one year by year (each year's change builds on the year before). Then it divides the construction index by the consumer price index. It shows the total real change since 1992 and the yearly rate. It has no verdict and no background colour, because the level depends on the choice of base year. Construction prices are for office buildings. The consumer price index is for a household basket.
- **Vacancy and employment.** This Insight shows two things on one chart. One is the yearly change in office vacancy, in percentage points. The other is the yearly growth of national employment, in percent. For employment we use two groups: information and communication (section J), and finance, real estate and business services (sections K to N). These are official groups of economic activity (NACE sections). We add the two employment groups together. We do not add vacancy and employment. The total starts in 2000 because section J has no earlier value. We leave out a year in which a section is not yet published. Only the vacancy change gets a verdict. The employment series covers the whole country, because no source publishes it for a City class. It is only a rough stand-in for office employment. In the small chart, vacancy is shown in % on the left axis and employment as an index (2015 = 100) on the right axis. The two axes have different units. So the crossings of the lines and the gaps between them carry no meaning, and the chart has no shading between the lines.
- **Vacancy and new completions.** The big number is the latest year's office completions as a percent above or below their average over the shared years. Completions are the floor space of new office and administrative buildings, for the whole country. The verdict and the background colour follow this number, and fewer completions is better. The small top chart shows something else: the yearly change in 127-town office vacancy, in percentage points, against the yearly growth of completions, in percent. The bottom chart shows vacancy in % on the left axis and completions as an index (2015 = 100) on the right axis. The two axes have different units, so crossings carry no meaning. Completions cover the whole country. They include public and owner-occupied buildings and exclude refurbishments and conversions.
- **Vacancy and rent growth.** The big number is the gap between the latest office vacancy in the 127 towns and its average over the period, in percentage points. The top chart shows this gap year by year. The period starts in the first year with a real rent growth rate, because that needs the year before. The bottom chart shows vacancy in % on the left axis and real rent growth in percent on the right axis. Real rent growth uses the real calculation above, on the rent index for new lettings. The rent index measures asking rents, not incentives such as rent-free months.
- **Verdict.** The tag compares the latest value with the average of the whole period, the latest year included. It says "in line with" when the latest value is within half the usual spread of the average (we use the population version). Otherwise it says "higher than" or "lower than". The grey band on the chart is the average plus or minus half the usual spread. The verdict only says where the latest value sits compared with the past. It does not mean values will move back to the average. It is not a cause and not a forecast.
- **Better and worse.** Some Insights also have a light green (better) or light pink (worse) background. Screen readers get the words. This is our own reading, from the point of view of an investor in office property. It is not a fact about the market, and it is not a cause or a forecast. For each Insight we say which direction counts as better. The background is green when the latest value is on the better side of the average, as the verdict judges it. It is pink when the value is on the other side. It stays plain when the verdict is "in line with" or when neither side is better. The sign of the number does not decide: a fall in vacancy is a negative change and is better, and a gap of -0.3 percentage points is higher than an average of -2.0 percentage points. Where the better direction is a close call, the table says so.
- **Growth charts.** Some big numbers are a change from the year before, or the gap between two such changes. For these, the small chart shows the changes as two lines on one axis, in percent. This covers three groups: real office rents vs real GDP; office rents vs construction costs; and real office rents and construction costs vs inflation. In the last group, we show nominal growth against consumer price inflation. Real growth is (1 + rent growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1, and for construction costs (1 + construction price growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1. So it differs slightly from the gap between the two lines. The gap between the two lines in the latest year is the big number, except for real office rents and real construction costs. For these two, the big number is the real growth. The shading between the lines is blue where the main line is above the comparison line and red where it is below.
- **Base year.** The remaining small charts show values. Where the two series have different units or bases, we show both as an index with the base year set to 100. This applies to three charts. Office prices vs rents: two indices with different base years, compared as a ratio. Office completions vs permits two years earlier: floor space, a ratio of two volumes. Office vacancy and office employment: the employment line. The base year is 2015, or the first shared year if a series has no value for 2015. 2015 is only a reference point (the year the ECB began buying assets). The one exception is the real construction cost index, which starts at the first year of its growth chart, 1992 = 100. The site does not claim that it caused anything. The size of the shaded gap between two lines set to 100 in the base year depends on this choice of base year. Series measured in percent or percentage points (vacancy rates, yields, interest rates) are never set to 100 in a base year. We show them in their own unit, on their own axis, beside any index.
- **Shift.** We compare completions with the permits issued two years earlier, because a permit takes about two years to become a completion. We move the permits series forward by two years and label it as such.

Which direction counts as better, per Insight:

| Insight | Better when the value is | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Initial yield spread over the 10-year Bund, by City class | higher | more income per unit of price than a government bond pays |
| Initial yield spread over the corporate loan rate | higher | more income per unit of price than the corporate loan rate costs |
| Office prices vs rents | neither | The number is a price-to-rent multiple, shown as an index (2015 = 100). When it is high, a buyer pays more per unit of rent and an owner holds more value. So it depends on whose side you take. |
| Real office rents | higher | rents growing faster than prices generally |
| Office rents vs construction costs | higher | rents growing faster than the cost of building |
| Real office rents vs real GDP | higher | Real rents grow faster than the real economy. This is a close call: it also means a rising cost burden for occupiers. It is an editorial judgement. |
| Office completions vs permits two years earlier | neither | Fewer completions per permit mean less new supply for owners but fewer projects for developers. It depends on whose side you take. |

Insight figures, including gaps, ratios, averages and series set to 100 in the base year, are our own calculation. We mark them as such in the source lines.

## How to read these figures

- We fetch each series either directly from its publisher or through DBnomics, which collects official sources. The source line says which. The publishers are not responsible for this site.
- For series fetched directly, the source line gives the date we last retrieved new data. It also gives the original unit (and, for the Bundesbank, the original title) as the publisher gave them. Our own titles, number formatting and rounding are not the publisher's.
- Germany construction totals, unit conversions, changes from the year before and Links are our own calculation. We mark them as such.
- The site shows only the latest published version of each series. It keeps no record of earlier revisions.
- The Deutsche Bundesbank commercial property series are based on data from other organisations (bulwiengesa, vdp).
- Publisher, series ID and licence for every series are listed under [Sources and licences](/en/sources).
